Home Office Deduction Guide for Freelancers: What You Can Actually Write Off

A practical breakdown of the simplified method vs. the regular method, and the exact square-footage math the IRS expects to see.

TT Tax Tools Editorial Team · August 18, 2026 · 8 min read · 638 words
Share:

If you run your freelance business from a spare bedroom, a converted garage, or even a dedicated corner of your living room, the home office deduction is one of the most valuable — and most misunderstood — write-offs available to self-employed taxpayers. Done correctly, it can shave thousands of dollars off your taxable income. Done carelessly, it can draw unwanted attention from the IRS. Here is exactly how it works.

Who actually qualifies

The home office deduction is reserved for people who are self-employed: freelancers, independent contractors, sole proprietors, and small business owners who file a Schedule C. If you are a traditional W-2 employee who happens to work remotely for a company, you do not qualify — that door closed for W-2 workers under current federal law, no matter how much of your living room your employer's monitor occupies.

To qualify, the space must pass two tests:

  • Regular and exclusive use. The area must be used only for business, consistently, not as a guest room that occasionally hosts your laptop.
  • Principal place of business. It must be where you primarily conduct administrative or management activities, even if you meet clients elsewhere.

Simplified method vs. regular method

The IRS gives you two ways to calculate the deduction, and choosing the right one depends on your space and your paperwork tolerance.

The simplified method multiplies your office's square footage (up to 300 square feet) by a flat rate. It is fast, requires almost no recordkeeping, and avoids depreciation recapture complications later if you sell your home.

The regular method instead calculates the business-use percentage of your entire home and applies it to actual expenses: mortgage interest or rent, utilities, homeowners insurance, repairs, and depreciation. It usually produces a larger deduction for people with big houses and small offices, but it demands real receipts and a clean paper trail. Mistakes that invite scrutiny

The two most common errors are claiming a space that doubles as a family room, and rounding square footage upward without measuring. Keep a simple floor plan with dimensions in your records, and take a dated photo of the space. Frequently asked questions

Can I claim the home office deduction if I work remotely for a company?

No. Under current law, W-2 employees cannot claim the home office deduction, even if their employer requires remote work. It is reserved strictly for self-employed individuals filing a Schedule C.

Do I need to use an entire room, or can it be part of one?

A clearly defined portion of a room can qualify as long as it is used regularly and exclusively for business — a desk area separated by a room divider, for example — but the space cannot double as personal-use space.

Will claiming a home office increase my audit risk?

Claiming a legitimate, well-documented home office is not inherently risky. Problems arise from vague measurements, mixed personal/business use, or deductions that are disproportionate to reported income.
